A general manager is the person responsible for the overall result of a clearly bounded business inside a larger organization, such as a geography, product, customer segment, or business line with its own profit-and-loss (P&L) statement. The role is often described as a mini-CEO: the GM sets the goal, owns the plan and tradeoffs, and brings together the work across product, operations, marketing, finance, analytics, and partnerships.

That promise has made general managers a popular answer to organizational ambiguity. A new product lacks momentum? Name a GM. A geography needs to grow? Name a GM. Two functions keep arguing? Put a GM between them and hope the title wins.

The model is most useful when the business has enough scale and cross-functional complexity that no single function can optimize the whole. A good GM can give a large company the focus and speed of a much smaller one. A bad setup adds one more person who owns a number but controls none of the work that produces it.

AI is also pushing general-management skills beyond formal GM roles.

One capable operator can now take a meaningful first pass at research, analysis, design, marketing, support, and software. That expands what one person can execute. It does not make the underlying choices easier: which customer to serve, which bets to make, how the economics should work, and where to spend the next week.

Inside a large company, a GM integrates those choices across functions. For a solo founder, they are the job. Yet while product management has been documented, debated, and turned into an industry, general management remains less understood.

This post is for anyone who is a GM, wants to become one, or is helping an organization decide whether to adopt the model.

I was a GM at DoorDash from 2019 to 2021, where I learned much of what I know about the model from Tony Xu and Christopher Payne. Since then, I have advised dozens of companies on the model, working with CEOs, COOs, board members, and other leaders at companies including Block and AngelList.

The title is the easy part. A GM model is an operating contract: one person gets a coherent business, a measurable outcome, committed cross-functional support, and real decision rights.

The failures I see are usually preventable, and they tend to show up in five places.

What follows distills the advice I give most often on how to address each one.

## Before You Appoint a GM

If you are considering a GM model, answer these five questions in writing:

1. **Is there a coherent business that actually needs a GM?** The unit should have a clear boundary, distinct customers or economics, several meaningful levers, and enough complexity that functions can each make sensible decisions while the business loses. If the GM will own a P&L, define its boundaries here.
2. **Can you define success in one long-term outcome with guardrails?** Leadership should agree on the target, with finance validating the economics and analytics validating the baseline and measurement. Neither team should be asked to bless a number after an executive has already announced it.
3. **Will the GM have committed partners and capacity?** Name the finance and analytics support, along with the product, engineering, design, operations, marketing, sales, and partnerships capacity the plan requires. A GM should not spend the job cleaning data or lobbying for every hour of functional help.
4. **Which decisions can the GM actually make?** Be explicit about pricing, priorities, partnerships, operations, and budget. Be equally explicit about what remains central.
5. **What happens when priorities collide?** Define the shared goals, review cadence, executive sponsor, and escalation path before the first real conflict.

If those answers are vague, I would not appoint the GM yet. Fix the model first.

The rest of this piece explains why each question matters and how to answer it.

## Give the GM a Real Business to Own

The first design decision is where the business begins and ends. A GM cannot own "growth" or "customer experience" in the abstract. The boundary needs to be specific enough that one person can be accountable for the result while still operating within the parent company's strategy and constraints.

The role becomes useful when functional ownership starts to break down. Marketing can hit its acquisition target while the product loses users. Product can ship its roadmap while unit economics get worse. Operations can improve quality while costs make the business impossible to scale. Each function can make a sensible decision while the business loses.

The GM exists to integrate the whole.

The GM model developed around the problem of where to draw that business boundary. It became useful when companies grew too complex to coordinate through either one functional hierarchy or a loose federation of businesses.

Go back to General Motors in 1920. William Durant had assembled GM from a sprawling collection of carmakers and parts suppliers. The businesses could move independently, but headquarters could not reliably compare their performance, control inventory, or decide where the next dollar of capital should go. [GM's 1922 annual report](https://digital.library.mcgill.ca/images/hrcorpreports/pdfs/G/General_Motors_Corporation_1922.pdf) documents the resulting inventory crisis. The loose federation no longer worked.

[Alfred Sloan's organization plan](https://books.google.com/books?id=qHJEAAAAIAAJ), formally adopted in late 1920 and effective in January 1921, did not pull every decision back to headquarters. It clarified how the operating divisions related to the center. The main car divisions were organized by brand and product line, not geography or individual model: Chevrolet, Oakland, Oldsmobile, Buick, and Cadillac.

Each was a largely self-contained operating unit with its own engineering, manufacturing, and sales functions, led by a general manager or divisional president responsible for its performance. Over the next several years, GM gave the brands distinct price positions, turning businesses that had often competed with one another into a more coordinated portfolio.

By the mid-1920s, the autonomy was real but bounded. Headquarters set company and product policy, controlled cash, approved major capital commitments, imposed common accounting and forecasting, and compared divisions on profit and return on investment. Committees coordinated work across engineering, purchasing, and sales. The division heads had broad operating authority, while headquarters controlled the decisions that affected GM as a whole.

Retail created the same need at a different level. Imagine Walmart trying to manage an enormous assortment from the center. Senior leadership can decide the company's strategy, but it cannot give sustained attention to every department and category. Who is waking up every day thinking about each one?

In [*Sam Walton: Made in America*](https://books.google.com/books/about/Sam_Walton_Made_in_America.html?id=JiKBEQAAQBAJ), Walton described Walmart's "Store Within a Store" system. Department heads were treated as managers of their own businesses, with visibility into the cost of goods, freight costs, profit margins, and how their department ranked against the same department in other stores. This was not a modern category GM with a chain-wide P&L, but it applied the same principle: push information and accountability toward the people closest to the work.

Now scale the same logic from a store department to a category. Take patio furniture as a hypothetical example. Someone needs to integrate the decisions about selection, inventory, pricing, and promotion because each one changes the economics of the others. Someone also has to react when a wet spring ruins the original forecast. Treating the category as a business gives those choices a common owner and a common economic model.

Amazon applied a similar logic as it grew from books into many categories. An Amazon [category leader](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/workplace/q-a-with-ayesha-harper) owns growth across multiple functions and is explicitly described as the general manager of the business.

The pattern now appears across technology companies, with the boundary drawn in different ways. [Intuit](https://www.intuit.com/company/executives/) has EVP/GMs for its Consumer, Small Business and Mid-Market, and Services groups.

The geographic model is one I know firsthand: I was the first New York City GM at [Caviar](https://www.trycaviar.com/home) from 2015 to 2017. Across ridesharing, food delivery, and other fast-growing categories of the 2010s, putting a GM in each market became a core organizational choice for ambitious companies expanding city by city: [Uber's early city GMs](https://www.uber.com/in/en/newsroom/gmuber/) were responsible for growth, marketing, operations, rider and driver support, supply, and service quality.

[Robinhood](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1783879/000178387922000162/hood-20220729.htm) applies the same logic to business lines rather than cities. In 2022, the company reorganized into a GM structure, placing product teams within businesses whose GMs had broad responsibility. A later [SEC response](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1783879/000178387923000248/filename1.htm) identified the initial businesses as Brokerage, Crypto, Say, and Cash, and its [2025 annual report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1783879/000178387926000023/hood-20251231.htm) says it remains organized around GMs. Christopher Payne, one of the people I learned from at DoorDash, [joined Robinhood's board](https://investors.robinhood.com/board-member/christopher-payne) in December 2024, after the reorganization was already in place.

These companies draw the boundary differently: by category, business line, or geography. Scale alone, however, does not create a need for GMs.

[Apple is a useful counterexample](https://www.apple.com/jobs/pdf/HBR_How_Apple_Is_Organized_For_Innovation-4.pdf). When Steve Jobs returned, he dismantled the business-unit structure, put Apple under one P&L, and organized it by function. The company instead aligned important decisions with deep functional expertise.

If a company's advantage depends on tightly integrated technology, design, and functional expertise, splitting it into separate mini-businesses may destroy more than it creates. A useful boundary has distinct customers, economics, or operating conditions. It also has real cross-functional tradeoffs, measurable results, and enough scale to warrant sustained attention. There is no universal revenue or headcount threshold.

Add GMs too early and the company fragments into tiny kingdoms. Add them too late and the same cross-functional decisions recur without anyone owning the aggregate result.

Once the unit is coherent, the next job is to make success measurable.

## Build the Model Behind the Goal

A GM needs a clear, long-term goal. It might be annual orders in a market, contribution profit for a business line, or active customers for a product. The metric depends on the business, but it should describe an outcome rather than a pile of work.

The goal also needs guardrails. Telling a team to increase orders without constraints can produce a lot of unprofitable orders. A useful goal pairs the primary outcome with the economics and customer experience that must remain healthy along the way.

The GM should not choose the number alone. Executives approve the target and capital commitment. Finance validates the economics. Analytics validates the baseline and measurement, helps estimate how different levers could move the result, and designs the tests. The GM owns the operating plan. These roles should align before the goal is announced, then revisit it as results come in.

The GM still owns the math. A good GM understands the data deeply enough to challenge it and make decisions from it. But understanding the data is different from spending half the job cleaning exports, reconciling dashboards, or writing every query. If that is the setup, the company has hired a GM before building the management infrastructure the role requires.

The Robinhood example shows why this agreement needs to be explicit. When the company reorganized around GMs, it did not yet have sufficiently precise and timely business-level financial reporting, so it had to [build the processes and controls](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1783879/000178387924000054/hood-20231231.htm) to produce it. By [2025](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1783879/000178387926000023/hood-20251231.htm), those processes existed, but Robinhood said its CEO still did not use GM-level financials to allocate resources or assess performance. A GM does not always need a standalone P&L, but the company should decide up front which business-level information will guide the GM and executive team.

Once the target and the model behind it are credible, the GM can turn them into a strategy. Start by listing the things that could materially move the outcome.

Depending on the business, those bets might be product changes, partnerships, marketing campaigns, operational capabilities, pricing moves, or user-experience improvements. Estimate their impact, write down the assumptions behind each estimate, and rank the three to five bets with the best combination of potential, confidence, cost, and time to learn. For each one, decide what evidence would raise or lower your confidence before you fully fund it.

Now return to our hypothetical patio-furniture GM. Imagine the category sold 100,000 items this year and the goal is 150,000 next year, without destroying margin or creating a warehouse full of markdowns. A large promotion before summer might add volume but reduce margin. Lower-priced products might attract new customers but introduce quality problems. A broader selection might improve conversion but create inventory risk. Better in-stock rates could help every other initiative.

The first forecast will be wrong. That is fine. Its job is to make the team's beliefs visible. If the plan only accounts for 25,000 of the 50,000 additional sales, the strategy is incomplete. If every bet depends on the same untested assumption about price, all of them may fail together. If the model reaches the goal only by ignoring returns and markdowns, the business is borrowing confidence from bad math.

The review rhythm should follow the same logic. An annual or multi-year goal sets the direction. Quarterly plans identify the few bets that matter now. Weekly or monthly business reviews, depending on the speed of the business, compare the model with reality. When the team falls behind, it should be able to say which assumption failed, what it learned, and what it will change.

A review that produces better slides but no different decisions is work theater with catering.

Even a sound plan is useless if the GM lacks the authority and resources to act on it.

## Give the GM Levers, Not Just a Target

A new GM role enters a company where formal and informal authority already exist. One function often has disproportionate influence, shaped in part by the founder's own discipline. At Square, now Block, I saw design carry unusual weight. At DoorDash, where delivering food created enormous operational complexity, [Strategy & Operations](https://careersatdoordash.com/blog/strategy-operations-doordash-2024/) did.

Before adding GMs, map where authority already lives. Who can change the roadmap? Who controls headcount and budget? Who approves a promotion or partnership? Whose judgment wins when speed, quality, revenue, and technical risk conflict? The honest answers matter more than the org chart.

This is where many GM models fail. A company installs a GM over an existing product organization but leaves the operating model unchanged. The GM carries the business target, while the PM is measured on a separate roadmap. Functional leaders allocate people without committing capacity to the GM's plan. Finance builds a budget the GM cannot influence. Executives continue to resolve every disagreement.

That person is a GM in name only. Accountability without authority is just a person to blame.

The GM's authority and resources need to match the goal. If pricing is central to the plan, who decides pricing? If the strategy depends on a partnership, can the GM negotiate it? If product changes account for half the forecast, which product, design, and engineering capacity has actually been committed?

A matrix can work without every functional partner reporting to the GM. It still requires those partners to treat the shared goal, resource commitments, and agreed decision rights as real. Functional leaders and the executive team have to uphold the contract after priorities collide. Approving the slide that describes it is not enough.

Central leadership will usually retain company-wide policies and tradeoffs around capital allocation, brand, risk, shared technology, and talent standards. The degree of delegation depends on the model. Within those boundaries, the GM needs enough room and committed capacity to run the business. Hitting a local target by pushing costs or problems into another business is not success.

In product-led software, one matrix relationship deserves its own treatment: product.

## Put the GM and PM on the Same Scoreboard

At DoorDash, GMs and product managers were paired around the same goals, even though the PM did not report to the GM. The GM remained accountable for the overall business, including operations, partnerships, marketing, and other non-product levers. The PM owned the product strategy and roadmap with design and engineering. Both were expected to think beyond their formal scope.

They did not have the same job. They did have the same scoreboard. That changed the conversation. When a monthly review showed that the business was behind, the GM could not simply blame the roadmap, and the PM could not point to a list of shipped features. They had to work together on the next best action.

The GM brings context about the whole business. The PM brings deep judgment about users, product quality, sequencing, and what software can realistically change. They are thought partners working at different levels of the same problem.

That division of responsibility still needs to be written down: how business priorities enter roadmap planning, which product decisions belong to the PM, how committed capacity can change, and which executive breaks a true deadlock. Otherwise the matrix works only when the two people already agree.

Without those rules, the GM plans around product capacity that never arrives and the PM ships work disconnected from the business model.

## Hire for Range and Ownership

Design the role before selecting the person. The GM job rewards a particular kind of generalist: someone who can move from a financial model to a customer complaint, from a product tradeoff to a supplier negotiation, and still remember which decisions actually matter.

Consulting firms such as Bain and McKinsey can produce strong candidates for GM-track roles because they teach structured thinking, analysis, and communication across unfamiliar industries. But analysis is preparation, not proof. Look for evidence that the person has led people, made decisions with incomplete information, and taken responsibility for the result.

When I hire or advise on hiring GMs, I use [founder mode](https://paulgraham.com/foundermode.html) as shorthand for intensity, tenacity, depth, a willingness to cross organizational boundaries, and a bias toward action. A hired GM still operates inside the company's strategy and constraints. The phrase is a hiring lens, not permission to ignore the rest of the company.

The best GMs combine urgency with evidence. They go to the lowest level of detail when the detail can change the decision. They act before every uncertainty is resolved, but make their assumptions explicit and test the risky ones quickly. They can persuade people who do not report to them, delegate real authority to specialists, and take responsibility when the plan fails.

A good generalist has enough depth in each function to ask useful questions, connect its work to the larger system, and recognize when specialist judgment should win. The role forces people to learn the economics of the business and, where applicable, P&L management. A good GM learns to see a business as a system rather than a collection of departments.

That is why AI makes this skill more valuable. As execution gets easier, deciding how the pieces fit together becomes a larger share of the job, whether or not the GM title exists.

Inside a company, when the operating contract is real, a GM can restore focus that scale tends to erode. When it is not, the new title only gives the old confusion someone else's name.
